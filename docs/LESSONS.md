# Lessons

Every rule below was written after a real mistake. The specifics here are **invented** —
domains, numbers, and identifying details are fictionalized on purpose (see the note at
the bottom) — but the *mechanism* of each failure is real and preserved exactly, because
the mechanism is what makes the rule worth following. A rule stripped to a bare statement
("don't fabricate," "verify your sources") is forgettable. A rule with a specific, vivid
failure attached to it is the thing that actually stops you from repeating it.

---

## 1. Confirm the posting is alive before you invest an hour

A full evaluation — scorecard, company research, due-diligence questions, the works — was
once written for a role that had already closed. The job board listing was still up; the
employer's own careers page had pulled it weeks earlier. The tell was there the whole time:
the original link 404'd on the employer's own domain, and that was read as a glitch rather
than a signal.

**The rule:** before spending real time on a role, confirm it's live on the employer's own
ATS, not just wherever you found it. A dead link on the employer's own site is a fact, not
an inconvenience to route around.

## 2. Never infer compensation from a title

A role was passed on early because its title ("Manager") suggested a compensation level
below the floor. Nobody actually looked up real market data for that title at that
employer. Weeks later, someone did — the verified band topped out nearly $80,000 above
where the guess had put it, comfortably clearing the floor. The Pass had been wrong, and it
had been wrong for a checkable reason.

**The rule:** reject on comp only when a *visible, verified* band's entire top is clearly
under your floor. An inferred number — from title, seniority language, or reporting line —
is not a verified number, and title correlates with pay far more loosely than instinct
suggests.

## 3. The biggest title in a group is not evidence of the most authority

A sourcing sweep across a cluster of similar roles ranked one candidate role #1, partly
because it carried the most senior-sounding title in the group. Its actual job description
stated — three separate times, in three different sections — that the role did *not* own
strategy, prioritization, or business outcomes for its own function. The largest title in
the cluster concealed the least real authority in it, and a summary-line read missed what a
full-text read would have caught immediately.

**The rule:** title tells you almost nothing about authority on its own, in either
direction. Score authority from what a job description actually says about team size,
budget, and decision rights — read the whole document, not the headline.

## 4. A non-response is a rejection after about two weeks — and no follow-up

Simple, but it compounds: an "awaiting response" pipeline that never gets pruned makes
your active list look bigger and busier than it is, and chasing silent employers rarely
changes an outcome. Move stale rows to "passed" with a dated note; nothing is deleted, and
a late callback costs nothing to reactivate.

## 5. Search by vocabulary, not by industry, when your target role crosses industries

A role type assumed to exist mainly in one industry turned out to have live, well-paying,
fully remote matches across several unrelated industries once the search stopped filtering
by industry and started matching the *actual language* of a strong-fit job description —
its specific phrases for scope, technology, and working style. The industry-first search
had been quietly excluding most of the real matches the whole time.

**The rule:** when your ideal role isn't industry-specific, search by the fingerprint
vocabulary of a role you know is a strong match, not by the industries you assume it lives
in.

## 6. The friction beat in a cover letter is where fabrication happens — and why

A cover-letter structure that works well for sounding authentic asks for a moment of real
friction: something that didn't work at first, and what changed. This is good writing
advice, and it is also, reliably, where invented details creep in — because when a genuine
documented failure isn't on hand, the pressure to produce *something* small, specific, and
mildly self-deprecating gets resolved by inventing one. An invented detail in this slot is
*more* convincing than a true one, not less, because specificity reads as authenticity
regardless of whether it's earned.

This happened, independently, in two different letters for the same person, in the same
structural position each time. In one, a letter claimed a specific comparative result
between two parts of a system ("the forecasting model carried more of the result than the
anomaly-detector did") that nothing in the underlying fact base supported — nothing had
ever actually compared the two. In the other, a letter described an early prototype as
having already used a sophisticated four-part architecture that, in reality, only existed
in a *later* version of the system; the early prototype had been much simpler and
noticeably worse. Two different systems, two different points in time, merged into one
cleaner, more impressive, and false narrative.

**The rule:** the friction beat must cite a *documented* failure — something you can answer
three follow-up questions about without inventing anything: what specifically was tried,
how you know it didn't work, and what evidence shows what changed. If no such failure
exists for the story you want to tell, tell a different story. Never merge two systems or
two time periods into one narrative for cleanliness. And watch for the tell: a genuine
account of an expensive alternative usually stays **counterfactual** ("running the full
system on every case *would have* cost X") — the fabrication move is converting a
counterfactual into a claimed event ("I built it, and it cost X").

## 7. A number invented during drafting is the same failure, one layer deeper

The rule above was already in force — cite a documented failure — when it happened a third
time, in a different form. The *story* in the friction beat was accurate: a real,
documented early failure of a forecasting prototype. But mid-draft, the letter added a
specific number to make the moment land harder — a per-unit cost figure — and that number
actually belonged to a *different, later* system, not the one being described as having
failed. The narrative was true. One number inside it wasn't, and the number was the thing a
careful reader would have caught.

**The rule:** before writing the friction beat, pre-commit it as a small skeleton of facts,
each with a citation to where it lives in your fact base — including every number. No
number may enter the prose during drafting that wasn't already in that skeleton. The
pressure that produces a fabricated story also produces a fabricated number inside an
otherwise true story, and pre-committing the narrative alone does not stop the second
failure mode.

## 8. Never attribute a quote to a document you haven't verified it's actually in

A cover letter quoted a company's own language back to them — a strong move when it's
accurate, because it proves you actually read their posting. The quote was real company
language. It was from a *different, related* posting at the same company — a sibling role
on the same team — not the one being applied to. Both postings had been researched in the
same session and the language blurred together.

**The rule:** any phrase presented as "your posting says" must be verified against *that
exact document*, not a sibling one researched alongside it. When researching multiple
related postings together, label every quote with which one it actually came from at the
moment you capture it — that's the only point where the distinction is still easy to make.

## 9. A skill is claimable only if it's sourced — plausibility is not sourcing

Three separate times, a skill or credential crept into a resume or letter that had zero
support in the underlying fact base — each time because a job description mentioned it and
it seemed *plausible* the person probably had some familiarity with it. None of the three
were ever confirmed.

**The rule:** if a role wants something not in your fact base, that's real information
about fit — surface it honestly, or go verify it with the person before it ships. Never let
"they're probably fine at this" substitute for an actual source.

## 10. Never state a total count of things you've built

An early draft described a person's body of work as "three systems in production." The
person pushed back hard: there were significantly more than three, and stating a small
exact count undersold real breadth and invited exactly the wrong question ("is that all?").
Worse, some job descriptions explicitly score for breadth across use cases — a stated count
of three actively argues against the very qualification being evaluated.

**The rule:** convey range, not a tally. A construction like "from [smallest/most narrow
example] to [largest/most different example]" proves breadth in one sentence, needs no
number, and can't be undercut by a follow-up asking for the full list.

## 11. State the denominator whenever you call something universal

A portfolio document said "every project goes through the same review process" — true, and
also, with no stated scale, easy for a reader to infer a large organization when the real
scale was a handful of projects. A rule that's universal *within a small set* and a rule
that's universal *within a large one* are different claims, and omitting the size of the
set lets the reader fill in a bigger one than is real.

**The rule:** whenever you describe something as universal or standard practice, state the
size of the set it's universal over, in the same sentence.

## 12. Don't invent a reason for a number you didn't choose for that reason

A document described a specific threshold as deliberately chosen "rather than a round
number" — except the number in question *was* a round number, and there had never been any
stated rationale for the threshold at all. The sentence invented a justification that a
careful reader could disprove by doing arithmetic in their head.

**The rule:** separate numbers you were given (defaults, inherited constraints) from
numbers you actually derived and can explain. Claiming authorship of a default you didn't
choose costs more credibility than it buys, and a reader who catches one invented
rationale discounts every other number on the page.

## 13. Recompute a derived number against its real endpoint — don't reuse an old one

A resume described "seventeen years" of experience in a sentence that, when checked against
the actual start date and the document's own stated timeframe, worked out to eighteen. The
"seventeen" figure had been accurate a year earlier and was carried forward unchanged.

**The rule:** any number derived from a date (years of experience, time since a milestone)
gets recomputed against its actual current endpoint every time it's used, not copied from
the last document that used it.

## 14. Domain-adjacent experience is not a flat gap — but don't overclaim its scope

Someone with deep experience in one regulated domain had also built a real, substantial
side project in a *different* regulated domain — genuinely relevant experience, initially
scored as a total gap because it didn't come from a full-time role. Once surfaced properly,
it became a real differentiator. But the correction almost overcorrected: the temptation
was to pair it with a large employer's scale and let the reader assume equivalent scale.
The honest version named the real boundary — a small, real project, not an enterprise
deployment — before a reader had to ask.

**The rule:** genuine domain-adjacent experience deserves to be claimed fully and
specifically. It does not license borrowing the scale of whatever employer you're pairing
it with. Name the actual scope, including its limits, in the same breath you claim the
credential.

## A note on how this document was written

This tool was built from a real, hand-run, confidential job search. Every lesson above
corresponds to a real mistake that search made. The company names, exact figures, domains,
dates, and req identifiers throughout this document are **entirely invented** — none of the
numbers are real measurements, and none of the "systems" or "roles" described are real
systems or real postings. Only the *shape* of each failure is preserved, because the shape
is the reusable part. If any detail here happens to resemble something specific, that's
coincidence, not a hidden reference.
